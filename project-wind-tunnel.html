Desktop Wind Tunnel

A student-built suction wind tunnel used to compare aerodynamic test models through ANSYS Fluent simulations and physical testing.

Test Models & Comparative Analysis LeadSOLIDWORKSANSYS FluentFDM 3D Printing

Project scope and my role

I chose the comparative-analysis role because I wanted a project where I could learn ANSYS Fluent while working on aerodynamics. Formula 1 and high-performance cars heavily influenced the test-model idea, so the project included both airfoils and vehicle models instead of treating the tunnel as a single demonstration.

My core responsibility was to select and model test geometries, run CFD before physical testing, compare results, and help manufacture the tunnel. Beyond that role, I supported additional CAD and assembly, handled many of the long FDM prints, and contributed to the mechanical drag-measuring rig and test-model mounting system.

Test-model selection

The tunnel had a test-section constraint of approximately 5.7 × 5.7 × 9.5 in. I used those dimensions to shortlist models that would fit without blocking too much of the flow.

  • Vehicles: a McLaren F1 road-car model and a Volkswagen Beetle because they represent very different approaches to vehicle shape and aerodynamic performance. A Formula 1 race-car model was also considered as an additional comparison.
  • Airfoils: NACA 0012, 2412, 4412, 0015, 2415, and 4415, with additional angle-of-attack testing planned around 0012 and 4412.
  • Spheres/textures: ping-pong, golf, and pickleball shapes as options for comparing surface effects and wake behavior.

Airfoil modeling workflow

I generated NACA coordinates, reformatted them in Excel, imported the points into SOLIDWORKS and ANSYS, and scaled the physical models to fit the test section. In SOLIDWORKS, the final printed airfoil span was 2 in with a chord of about 3.94 in.

ANSYS Fluent setup

To keep the comparison consistent, I created a reusable CFD workflow for each airfoil rather than rebuilding the simulation from scratch.

Geometry: place the test model inside a fluid-domain representation of the test section and subtract the solid model from the fluid volume.
Named selections: define inlet, outlet, and the airfoil wall so the same boundary setup can be reused.
Mesh: apply 0.002 m face sizing at the airfoil wall plus inflation with 10 maximum layers and a 1.2 growth rate.
Solver: steady, pressure-based Fluent solution using air and the k-ω SST turbulence model.
Boundary conditions: 3.6895 m/s velocity inlet, 5% turbulence intensity, turbulence viscosity ratio of 10, and a 0 Pa pressure outlet.
Reports: compute drag in the x-direction and lift in the y-direction after 500 iterations.

Airfoil comparison results

The preliminary zero-degree simulations followed physically reasonable trends: the symmetric sections produced nearly zero lift, while increased camber produced more positive lift and generally more drag in this setup.

How I interpret these results: at the same 12% thickness, moving from NACA 0012 to 2412 to 4412 increased lift from approximately zero to 0.0368 to 0.0744, while drag also increased. The same general trend appeared in the 15% series. Because this is a small, low-Reynolds-number student-built system, I treat the values as preliminary model-scale CFD results rather than validated aerodynamic data. Experimental calibration and CFD-to-wind-tunnel comparison are still being refined.

FDM manufacturing and fit iteration

I helped choose print settings that balanced stiffness, mass, print time, and filament use. Most tunnel parts were printed in PLA with 15% gyroid infill, 0.16 mm layer height, and four walls.

The modular connection geometry was tested before committing to large prints. A 0.20 mm clearance was too loose, 0.15 mm was still loose, and the design was tightened to 0.10 mm for the final connection approach.

Physical testing and current status

The tunnel uses the fan downstream to pull air through the intake, flow straightener, contraction, test section, and diffuser. The test section supports interchangeable models and a mechanical force-measurement setup. The system is functional but still being refined; calibration, force measurement, and CFD-to-experiment comparison remain part of the ongoing iteration.
